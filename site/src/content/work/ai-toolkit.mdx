## The problem: coordination by hand

Keeping four teams moving means answering the same questions over and over. The answers lived in hundreds of tickets, and the only way to get them was to read the tickets.

Three questions hurt the most:

- **"Which team has room?"** When new work came in, there was no quick, reliable answer.
- **"Are these the real numbers?"** Status reports and engineering metrics were put together by hand at the end of every two-week sprint, copied between dashboards, spreadsheets, slides and wiki pages. Every copy was a chance for the numbers to drift out of sync.
- **"What actually needs testing?"** Each release, after the release candidate was cut, someone had to go through the finished items, decide which had real user-facing changes worth acceptance testing, then change each one's status and assign it for testing by hand.

My users were my own teams and leadership, so I treated it like any other product: who's it for, and how do I know it's right?

## Digging in: do it by hand first

I did every task by hand with the AI before I automated it. Each time I corrected it, the correction became a rule. That's error analysis, the slow way, and it's how I learned what the job actually was.

For capacity, my goal was simple: anyone should be able to see at a glance how much each team has on its plate and which ones have room to take on new work.

My first attempt is the cautionary tale: a spreadsheet that needed four manual data pulls every time I refreshed it. That's not a quick answer to "which team has room?" It's homework. The managers already kept a staffing spreadsheet, so version two read that directly, plus live workload from the tracker.

## Constraints

- **Trust.** Nobody wants an AI quietly editing their tickets. So ==the AI had to earn the right to make changes.== Each workflow started out drafting while I approved. I only let it update tickets itself (status, story points, fix version and more) after a few test runs where I confirmed the edits were right and nothing else broke. When it got something wrong, that became a new rule. (One example: Jira descriptions have to be written in ADF, Jira's own format, not Markdown.) It still shows me every change before it makes it, so I can spot-check a whole batch in a minute instead of going ticket by ticket, and every edit shows up in the ticket's history.
- **The tracker fights back.** Its own automation would overwrite ticket content, so the ticket-drafting workflow had to create tickets in a way that keeps that from happening.
- **No new chores.** A tool that needs people to maintain one more sheet is a tool that goes stale.
- **Approved tools only.** The company had already standardized on Claude and approved connecting it to our tracker, wiki and code, so I built on that instead of wiring up something new.
- **I still had a day job.** I built this while running releases.

## What I did

I built 10 workflows and 7 dashboards across release management, capacity and routing, reporting, team rituals and hiring. The biggest one, the release risk review, [gets its own case study](/work/release-risk-review/). Here's the rest, and the calls behind it.

**Use the source of truth people already keep.** The live capacity report combines the managers' staffing sheet with live tracker workload. It shows each team's load by stage (development, code review, QA, product) and by type of work, as a full report or a quick routing recommendation. This one was less about saving time than fairness: before, some teams were buried while others were short on work, and some work sat unassigned because nobody claimed it. The tradeoff: it's only as current as their sheet. I'll take that over a new sheet nobody updates.

**Verify before trusting.** Acceptance-testing triage scans completed work and recommends the items with real user-facing changes, filtering out backend-only and test-automation work. Before anyone relied on it, I ran it on the same releases I'd triaged by hand, including the past three, and compared its picks to mine item by item. I refined the rules until it caught everything I caught. Early on it recommended items it shouldn't have, and each of those became a new rule. Now, of the roughly 16 items it recommends in a release, maybe one or two shouldn't be there (my estimate). That's the cheap mistake: a tester spends a few minutes and moves on. The expensive one is leaving off a real user-facing change, and it hasn't done that yet. I also reconcile dashboards against my own hand counts.

**One status, three places, checked.** Updating one initiative's status used to mean checking several sources, copying between them, writing it up, then updating a slide and a wiki page, and we usually have 7–10 initiatives going. Now I update one source of truth, and the workflow updates the slide and the wiki from it, then checks that all three agree.

The small stuff matters too. Picking sprint demos used to take me about two hours of combing through last sprint's tickets. Now a workflow picks what's worth demoing and assigns presenters within each team, never the person who built it, and it goes through everyone before anyone gets a second turn. The team sees it as fair, which is more than most rotation spreadsheets can say.

For the record, the first thing I built wasn't any of this. It was a skill that turns my loose weekly recap into 1-on-1 notes for my manager. A little selfish, I know.

## What happened

- **10 reusable workflows and 7 dashboards** for four engineering teams and their managers. The team's feedback has been good: it saves them time, and releases go smoother, with fewer SEV calls and more time on planned work instead of putting out fires.
- **75% less sprint reporting time,** measured in hours spent before vs. after. Sprint metrics are now rebuilt automatically instead of copied and pasted.
- **114 → 16.** In one release, acceptance-testing triage reviewed 114 items and recommended 16. Its picks have held up for the past couple of releases, so it moves the tickets itself after a quick look from me.

The bigger win was what it found. The AI surfaced these, and I confirmed every one before taking it to anyone.

- 68 merged items in one release that were never tagged to it.
- 120 in-progress items with no team assigned at all.
- One team carrying several times its capacity while another had room.
- 3 features marked complete in the tracker that had never been built. I checked the code before writing the close-out, so the plan changed from "verify and close" to "build."
- Formula errors in one project tracker that were understating the team's progress.

The cycle time report showed the biggest delay was work waiting for QA, a median of 7.3 days, and only about 1 in 5 items finished within its estimate-based time budget.

## Looking back

It's a product, so it has versions. I've improved several of these workflows over time and retired the old versions, and the newer ones are still in use. Ship version one, watch how people use it and don't get attached to it.
