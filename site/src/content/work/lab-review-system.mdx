## The problem: a backlog that only grew

Work leaving the lab went through two systems: the review system and a companion release tool. I owned the backlog for both.

The systems worked. They just weren't friendly. People got confused, hit the same errors and asked the same questions, and each of those became a backlog item. The developers who could fix them were shared, so our items kept getting pushed behind more urgent work. The backlog grew.

## Digging in: three signals

**"My record was submitted, right?"** Researchers believed they'd submitted records that never were. I walked the test site as a brand-new user, asking one question the whole way: "If this were my first time using this, how would I know what to do next?"

You wouldn't. The "Start Manager Review" button didn't appear until every required field was filled in, and nothing told you a button was coming. Nothing warned you when you left a half-finished record. And the record's status said "New," which sure sounds like it worked.

**The same questions, every day.** We tracked the recurring questions and talked them through in our daily meetings. The systems had no user guides, and new employees had it worst.

**Feedback and errors with nowhere to go.** I consolidated the scattered backlog and ranked it by user feedback and how often each issue came up.

## Constraints

- **Old system, small budget.** Changes had to stay minimal.
- **Borrowed developers.** They had little time for our department.
- **Access was its own project.** Getting into anything ourselves meant a whole process.

## What I did: the smallest fix for the biggest pain

Every item got the same test: ==what's the smallest change that makes this pain go away?==

**1. Incomplete records: three changes, not a redesign.** A rebuilt submission flow would have been nicer, and it would have sat in the developer queue forever. So:

- The "Start Manager Review" button is always visible, grayed out until the record is ready.
- The status changed from "New" to "Unsubmitted Draft."
- Leaving an incomplete record pops up a warning that changes may not save.

**2. The backlog, and the developer bottleneck.** A ranked backlog doesn't help if nobody can work it. I'd finished my B.S. in software engineering, so I shadowed a senior developer, earned access to the repo, Jira and Azure DevOps and started shipping fixes myself. Each one maps to a pain:

**3. Self-serve help (built with a co-worker).** The lab's training department was working on official courses, but users needed answers now. We chose speed over polish and used tools we already had:

- A department SharePoint homepage (I took point) with links to both systems, the FAQ, a process overview and our expedite request form.
- An FAQ, which I converted from a Word doc to a web page with a linked table of contents.
- A walkthrough video for each system, which my co-worker and I recorded together.

## What happened

- **Abandoned records:** records people left unsubmitted by accident dropped ==from hundreds per year to nearly zero==. Helpdesk emails about how to submit a record dropped dramatically, too.
- **Questions:** the number of questions decreased by approximately 50%. The walkthrough videos were adopted by multiple research departments for training new users.
- **Errors:** upload errors became nearly non-existent after I cut the upload limit to 120MB to match a federal repository's requirements.
- **Overall:** fewer user-reported issues, and fewer reviews that needed an admin to step in.

I don't have exact baselines or dates for these. They're what we saw, not what a dashboard measured.

## Looking back

Hard numbers were scarce here, which is exactly why I measure first now.

The biggest result on this page came from a status label and a grayed-out button. Before asking for a redesign, check whether the system is just telling people the wrong thing.
